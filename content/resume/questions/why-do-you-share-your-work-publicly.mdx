title: Why do you share your work publicly? Why build websites and make everything publicly available instead of using third-party publishing platforms?
order: 4
answer: |-
  I was deeply influenced by the open-source spirit of computer science: people increase the bandwidth of communication by making their work publicly available, so others can inspect it, build on it, and help one another more effectively. I publish my work in the hope that people with interests similar to mine can benefit from it, just as I have benefited from the public work of others.

  I also believe users should own their data and be able to move it freely across media, tools, and platforms. That is why I intentionally avoid making services such as WeChat Official Accounts, proprietary note applications such as Evernote, or hosted site builders such as Google Sites the source of truth for my work. Those environments can introduce intentional or unintentional barriers to exporting, restructuring, or migrating data. Instead, I prefer portable formats such as Markdown, version-controlled repositories on GitHub, and static websites that I can build, inspect, migrate, and host without depending on a social-media or publishing company.

  Most of what I share is a by-product of my daily practice rather than a commercial content strategy. It is personally satisfying, but it also serves as a record of learning: evidence that I studied something, an attempt to test whether I truly understand it, and a way to gain hands-on experience. I think of these projects as the wet lab of my computer-science education—places where abstract knowledge has to survive contact with implementation.

  There is also a practical communication reason. I dislike explaining the same context repeatedly. A public, durable, linkable body of work lets people review the relevant context before a conversation begins. That saves time and tokens for everyone and allows the conversation to start from a shared state rather than from zero.
